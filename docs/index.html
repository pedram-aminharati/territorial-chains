Does a synthetic population know where its people live?

Agent-based transport models start from a synthetic population in which every person carries a day of activities. This study measures whether those days differ between urban and rural residents the way they do in French travel surveys, using the open-source eqasim pipeline on Nantes and its hinterland. The answer turns out to depend on which survey is taken as the reference.

The question

eqasim builds a population from the census and gives each person the recorded day of a survey respondent who resembles them. This is statistical matching: the two people share sex, car ownership, age class, socio-professional class and, when enough respondents exist, département. A cell needs 20 respondents; below that, attributes are dropped starting from the end of the list.

Territory is last on that list. So the working hypothesis was simple: where respondents are scarce, which is exactly in small towns and rural communes, the place a person lives is the first thing the matching forgets.

A second question came up on the way and turned out to matter as much: against which survey should the population of one département be judged, the national survey split by type of territory, or the département's own survey?

Staying at home

The share of people who make no trip on a weekday is the simplest chain indicator and the one with the clearest territorial gradient in the national survey: 10.1% in suburbs against 16.8% in rural communes. The regional survey of Loire-Atlantique does not show that gradient: between 8.8% and 9.4% in every kind of place.

Which survey is right for Loire-Atlantique?

Up to here the populations are judged against the national survey, split by type of territory. Loire-Atlantique also has its own household travel survey, EDGT 2015, with 20,799 respondents. It was read through eqasim's own cleaning stage and each respondent was given the urban type of the home commune. On staying home, the two surveys disagree.

What this does and does not show. The surveys differ in more than place: seven years apart, and the regional survey was run face to face in the three agglomerations and by telephone elsewhere, so survey mode and territory are partly confounded in it. The regional survey also records more activities per person, which sets every population built from the national survey further from it. And the urban type of regional respondents comes from today's urban units, not from the survey file. What holds despite this is the direction: both samples from the département contradict the national gradient in staying home.

Distances: where the pipeline already works

Distance is the strongest territorial contrast in the surveys. In eqasim it does not come from the matched chain: work and school places are drawn from census commuting flows and the other activities are placed afterwards. That step keeps the gradient whatever the matching design.

Does it matter once the population is simulated?

Populations A (eqasim defaults) and C (urban type added) were each run through MATSim on the same road network, the same public-transport timetables and the same mode-choice parameters. Any difference between the two runs therefore comes from the activity chains. eqasim's own convergence rule stopped the runs after 60 and 57 iterations.

How stable is this?

Everything above comes from one random seed. Variants A and C were generated again with four other seeds, each redrawing the 10% census sample, the matching and the locations, and two of those were also simulated. That gives 5 populations and 3 simulations per variant. Dots are means over seeds; bars run from the lowest to the highest seed.

Can a generative model do better than matching?

Five families were trained on the same survey respondents and asked to propose a day for respondents they had never seen, from their attributes alone: eqasim's statistical matching, a Bayesian network, a small transformer that writes the chain one activity at a time, a variational autoencoder that first draws a summary of the whole day and then writes it, and a GAN that writes the whole chain at once. Each family was run with and without the home territory. The test was repeated over 5 random splits; dots are means and bars the range.

What the surveys say

Before judging a generator, the contrast has to be measured in the data. Both national surveys were read through eqasim's own cleaning code, weighted, and split by INSEE urban-unit status.

Method and reproducibility

Five populations were generated for Loire-Atlantique with eqasim-france v1.5.0 at a 10% sampling rate, then scored by one script against the survey they were matched from and, for the ENTD variants, against the regional survey as well. A synthetic person's territory is the urban-unit status of the home commune. Observed and generated chains go through the same code.

Two measures

  • Fidelity inside a territory: distance between the generated and observed distribution for people living in the same kind of place.
  • Contrast retention between territories: the survey says rural and urban days differ by some amount; the share of that difference found again in the population. A generator can look fine on average and fail this one.

How it builds on published work

  • Hörl and Balac (2021) describe the matching and validate chains by ranking the most frequent ones against the survey; the top-chain figure here does the same, by territory. Their paper also notes that the census gives residence only at département level for municipalities under 10,000 inhabitants, which is the origin of the small-town effect measured above.
  • The same paper handles sampling noise with repeated realisations over random seeds, reporting the mean and range. The Stability section does the same for variants A and C: five populations and three simulations each.
  • Joubert and de Waal (2020) generate chains with Bayesian networks and Shone and Hillel (2024) with deep generative models, judging output by its distribution and by how much of it is new. Both families are implemented here (a Bayesian network, a transformer, a variational autoencoder and a GAN) and scored against matching on held-out respondents, with repeated splits.

Limits

  • Two references are used and they disagree on staying home: the national survey by urban type (2008) and the regional survey (2015). Neither is the population's own year, 2022, and they differ in protocol.
  • Variants A and C have five seeds (three simulated). Variants B, D and E, the donor grids, the top chains and the maps show one seed.
  • In the variants with national donors, 23 to 105 matched persons belong to a household that leaves the pipeline without a home commune. They are left out of every table by territory; the cause has not been investigated.
  • The simulation uses mode-choice parameters calibrated for Île-de-France and current timetables. Its levels are not a forecast for Loire-Atlantique; only differences between variants are read.
  • Urban-unit status has four classes; a finer typology (density grid, accessibility) may show contrasts this one hides.
  • The EMP weighting result follows the rule in the statistical office's note on the public files, applied here to people; the note does not treat immobility itself.
  • In the simulations about 3% of planned trips are missing from the output. When the last iteration ends, about 4,800 agents have not finished their day and none was removed, which accounts for 95% of the persons concerned. Their last recorded trip starts around noon. What holds them up was not identified.
